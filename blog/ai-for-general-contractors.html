AI for General Contractors: Estimates, Subs, and Change Orders

Brady Gay · August 10, 2026

Where the money leaks on every GC job

You won every bid you have right now by being good at the work. The problem is the work around the work — the back-and-forth that happens before a shovel hits dirt and the follow-up that drags on after the last nail is set.

Three places bleed time and revenue on almost every general contracting job:

Estimates that go out and never come back. You spend real time pulling a number together. You send it. The prospect goes quiet. Nobody follows up at day three, day seven, or day fourteen because you are already on-site and your office is handling a dozen other things. The job eventually goes to another contractor — not because your price was wrong, but because you were not the last voice in their ear.

Subcontractor coordination that depends on whoever remembers to call. You have four subs scheduled for next week. One of them has not confirmed. The material delivery is contingent on that sub showing up Tuesday. You find out Friday afternoon when it is too late to do anything about it. That half-day of downtime is not a bad luck event. It is a coordination failure that was entirely preventable.

Change orders that get agreed to verbally and documented late — or never. The client asked for the window rough-out to move six inches. You said sure, noted it in your head, finished the framing. Three weeks later the invoice has a line item they do not recognize and now you are defending a charge for work they have already forgotten approving. That conversation costs you time and sometimes money.

None of these failures happen because your crew is not doing their job. They happen because the administrative layer of a GC operation is genuinely hard to staff. You need someone who is organized, responsive, and never on a job site when the phone rings. Most small GC shops do not have that person. They have you, a part-time office coordinator, and a lot of things that fall through the cracks.

Why estimate follow-up goes cold

Industry data on contractor sales cycles shows a consistent pattern: the contractor who follows up first and most consistently closes a higher percentage of bids — even when the competing estimate is lower. Prospects choosing a GC are making a trust decision as much as a price decision. Responsiveness signals organization. Organization signals that the job will go smoothly.

The follow-up problem is not that GCs do not know they should be doing it. It is that follow-up is precisely the kind of task that disappears during a busy week. You are managing an active job. Something broke. A sub has a question. Your phone has seventeen unread texts. Following up on an estimate you sent six days ago to a prospect you have not heard from is at the bottom of the list — until you find out they signed with someone else.

Your AI team owns estimate follow-up end to end. When an estimate goes out, the AI admin logs it and starts a follow-up sequence. Day two: a short, direct check-in — did the estimate land, any questions on scope or timeline? Day five: a second touch, still brief, no pressure. Day ten: a final check-in that offers to walk through the numbers if anything looks unclear. Each message goes out automatically. Each response gets routed to you with context, so when you do get on the phone with a prospect, you know exactly where the conversation left off.

You do not have to remember who you sent estimates to. You do not have to carve time out of a Wednesday afternoon to make calls. The follow-up happens because the AI admin is running the process, not waiting to be asked.

The real cost of subcontractor no-shows

A missed day on a construction job is not just an inconvenience. It is a cascade. If the plumber does not show Tuesday, the drywall crew scheduled for Thursday cannot come in. The drywall delay pushes electrical. The project timeline slips a week. The client asks questions. You absorb the schedule pressure, often without being able to bill for it.

The irony is that most sub no-shows are not malicious. The sub forgot. They double-booked. They did not have the start time confirmed in writing. A single confirmation call or text the day before would have caught it. But that call has to happen for every sub on every job, and it has to happen reliably — not only when someone on your team has a free ten minutes.

Your AI admin runs subcontractor confirmations as a standard part of job scheduling. When a sub is booked, the AI teammate logs the date, the trade, and the contact. The day before, it sends a confirmation — via text or email, however that sub prefers to communicate — with the job address, start time, and any relevant site access notes. If the sub does not confirm, it escalates to you with enough lead time to make a call or line up a backup. If the sub confirms but mentions a conflict, that surfaces immediately rather than the morning of.

This does not require you to build new systems. The AI admin works with your existing schedule — whatever you use to track jobs and subs. The process runs in the background, quietly. What you notice is that fewer Tuesdays get wrecked by a no-show you could have caught Monday afternoon.

Change orders: the paperwork everyone agrees to skip and then regrets

Change orders are awkward. The client asks for something small. You say yes because it is a good client and the change is minor. You do the work. You add the line to the invoice. Three weeks have passed and the client does not remember the conversation the way you do, or does not remember it at all.

This is not a dishonesty problem. It is a memory problem compounded by time and the fact that nobody wanted to slow down the job to fill out a form. The result is invoice disputes, slow-pay, and the kind of friction that turns a satisfied client into a difficult one — over something that cost $400 in labor and ten minutes to document at the time.

The pattern that actually works is documentation at the moment of approval, not at invoicing. When a change is agreed to verbally on-site, it needs to be written down before anyone moves on. Who approved it, what was agreed to, what it adds to the contract price, and when it was authorized.

Your AI admin can run change-order documentation through a simple on-site workflow. When you or your foreman identifies a change, you dictate or text a quick summary. The AI teammate drafts the change-order record, sends it to the client for a written acknowledgment — a reply-by-text yes is enough — and logs the approval with a timestamp. By the time the invoice goes out, every change order has a documented trail. The client sees a line item and remembers approving it because they have a text record. The dispute rate drops, not because the work was different, but because the paperwork caught up to the conversation in real time.

Inbound calls when you are always on-site

General contractors are not office workers. You are on a job or driving to one. When a new prospect calls your main number because they found you on Google or got a referral, they frequently reach voicemail. Industry research on trades service businesses consistently shows that a significant share of inbound leads call only one contractor — the first one who picks up. If voicemail is the first interaction, a meaningful percentage of those callers move on before you call back.

This is a coverage problem. You cannot be available to take sales calls while you are managing a job site. But the calls keep coming, and each one that goes to voicemail is a bid opportunity that may never develop.

Your AI teammate answers every inbound call, around the clock. It handles a real intake conversation — what kind of project, rough timeline, address, how they heard about you — and books an estimate appointment directly onto your calendar at a time that works with your job schedule. You get a summary of who called and what they need before you show up. You are not walking into the estimate call cold.

For existing clients who call with questions or status requests, the AI admin handles the high-volume, routine interactions: where does the job stand, what is the schedule next week, when does the next draw happen. Anything that requires your judgment gets routed with context. Your phone stops being a liability you have to manage and starts being a channel that works without your attention.

What daily operations look like when the AI admin is running

You get to the job site at 7 a.m. Before you arrived, your AI admin confirmed all three subs scheduled for the week. One came back with a conflict on Thursday — it flagged that for you with a suggestion to push the affected work to Friday. That is the first thing in your summary, not a surprise at 7:05 when the sub does not show.

Two estimate follow-ups went out automatically to prospects from last week. One of them replied with a question about the foundation scope. The AI admin flagged it and drafted a reply for your review. You read it, approve it in thirty seconds, and it goes out. That prospect books a call for this afternoon.

A homeowner called at 8:45 p.m. last night about a kitchen addition. The AI teammate took the intake, answered their questions about your process and typical timeline, and booked an estimate visit for Saturday morning. You have the intake summary in your queue. You did not have to call anyone back this morning.

Two change orders from last week are still waiting on written client acknowledgment. The AI admin sent a follow-up on each one yesterday. One client replied and it is logged. The other has not responded — it surfaced that for you so you can make a call before the invoice goes out.

None of this required you to manage a process. It happened because the AI admin is running the operation in the background, continuously, and surfacing only the things that genuinely need your decision.

Frequently asked questions

Can the AI admin actually handle subcontractor communication, or does it just send a generic reminder?

It handles real, specific communication tied to each job. When a sub gets a confirmation, it includes the job address, start time, contact on-site, and any access instructions you want to include. If the sub replies with a question or a conflict, the AI admin handles routine replies and routes anything that needs your input with full context. It is not a mass blast. It is structured coordination for each trade on each job.

What happens when a prospect calls with a complex project and the AI teammate cannot answer their questions?

The AI teammate handles intake — what the project is, rough scope, timeline, location, how they found you — and books the estimate visit. It does not try to quote the job or answer detailed technical questions. Anything beyond intake gets clearly routed to you with a summary of the conversation. The prospect is not left hanging. They know a callback or site visit is scheduled. You get the information you need before you make contact. The AI teammate knows where its job ends and yours begins.

We are a two-person operation. Is this built for firms our size?

This is built specifically for operations where the owner is the primary decision-maker and the administrative layer is thin or nonexistent. A two-person GC shop has the same inbound calls, the same estimate follow-up problem, and the same change-order friction as a ten-person operation — with fewer people to absorb it. The AI admin fills the gap that would otherwise require a full-time office coordinator. The fit is tightest when the bottleneck is not the quality of the work but the bandwidth to handle everything around it.
