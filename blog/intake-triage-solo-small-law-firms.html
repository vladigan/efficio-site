Intake Triage for Solo and Small Law Firms

Brady Gay · August 10, 2026

The intake specialist you cannot hire

Ask any solo personal injury attorney what the bottleneck in their practice is, and the answer will not be case volume. It will not be marketing spend. It will not be settlement negotiations or the calendar. The answer will be intake.

Every solo PI, family, or estate-planning attorney runs on the same math. Their marketing spend generates a certain volume of inbound calls per month. A large share of those calls are not real cases — wrong jurisdiction, wrong practice area, statute expired, no damages, cannot pay, the other side is judgment-proof, or the caller is not the person with the legal problem. Somewhere inside that volume there are a handful of matters that are worth taking. And there is nobody at the firm whose job it is to sort through all of it.

The attorney is in a deposition or in court. The paralegal is handling three other case files. The receptionist — if the firm has one — is answering calls, but has neither the training nor the authority to run a real intake conversation. So calls get taken as messages. Messages get returned when the attorney has a window, which is usually the next day or the day after that. By then, the caller has already talked to two other firms. The one that answered live and ran a real intake got the case.

The theoretical fix is to hire a dedicated intake specialist. Someone whose full-time job is to answer the phone, run the intake questionnaire, disqualify the wrong-fit cases, and get the qualified matters onto the attorney's calendar for a fit call. That role solves the problem completely. But at $50,000 to $70,000 in fully-loaded cost for a good one, plus benefits, plus a desk, plus the management overhead, a solo firm generating one to three qualified matters a week cannot make the math work. So they do without. And they lose cases they should have signed.

Why intake is the constraint (not marketing or case volume)

There is a temptation, especially among firms with the marketing spend to know their numbers, to treat every dropped intake as a marketing problem. Buy more search ads. Buy more direct-response television. Fill the top of the funnel harder. The logic being that if a certain percentage of calls disappear before they get converted, we should just generate more calls.

That logic breaks in two places. First, more inbound volume with the same intake capacity means a lower conversion rate, not a higher one — each individual caller gets less attention because more of them are hitting a saturated intake process. Second, more inbound volume raises your customer-acquisition cost per signed matter to a level that quickly stops making sense, especially in verticals where the marketing cost per raw lead is already high.

The real constraint is not lead volume. It is intake capacity. A solo firm that gets forty new-matter inquiries a month and converts eight of them is not being held back by needing more inquiries. It is being held back by not being able to give the other thirty-two inquiries the attention that would tell them whether the case is real or not.

You can see this in the numbers. Firms that run tight intake — live phone answer, structured intake conversation, same-day follow-up on qualified matters — convert two to three times better on the same lead volume as firms that let intake slip. The difference is not the quality of the attorney or the reputation of the firm. It is whether the intake process runs consistently or whether it depends on whoever happens to be near the phone.

What an AI intake-triage teammate does

Your AI team owns the intake process end to end. When a new call, form submission, or chat inquiry comes in, the AI teammate picks it up immediately — day, night, or weekend — and runs a structured intake conversation.

Here is what that conversation looks like in practice. The AI teammate identifies itself, explains that it is going to gather some information before scheduling time with the attorney, and asks the caller to walk through what happened. It captures the facts in the same order and with the same level of detail that a trained intake specialist would — parties involved, jurisdiction, timeline, damages or injuries if it is a PI matter, relationship and children if it is a family matter, decedent and existing documents if it is an estate matter.

Then it runs the fit checks. Is this in a jurisdiction the firm handles? Is the statute still open? Is there an active engagement with another firm that would need to be terminated first? Does the caller pass any conflicts checks against the firm's existing client base? Is the matter within the firm's practice-area scope, or is it a referral opportunity?

Based on those checks, the AI teammate either schedules a fit call on the attorney's calendar with the intake summary attached, sends a clean referral to a partner firm the practice works with, or politely tells the caller the firm cannot help and, when appropriate, points them to a bar-referral service. In every case, the caller gets a real answer immediately — not a "we'll call you back" that they know means nothing.

For matters that get scheduled, the attorney walks into the fit call already knowing the facts, the fit-check results, the caller's expectations, and any red flags the intake surfaced. The call is the attorney making a signing decision, not the attorney gathering information they should have had before the call started.

The fit versus wrong-fit distinction (and why disqualification is a feature)

There is a real reluctance among solo attorneys to say no to potential matters. Every call feels like a possible case, and the cost of declining a real case feels much higher than the cost of taking a fit call on a marginal one. So attorneys often say yes to fit calls they should have declined, and then spend forty-five minutes in a conversation that was never going to end in an engagement.

That reluctance is expensive. A solo PI attorney who takes twelve fit calls a week when three of them are actually fit-appropriate is losing seven-plus hours to conversations that were never going to convert. Multiply that across a year and you are looking at hundreds of hours of attorney time spent on matters that a proper intake would have disqualified.

An AI intake-triage teammate treats disqualification as a first-class outcome, not a failure mode. When a caller is not a fit — the statute expired six years ago, the matter is not in the firm's jurisdiction, there is a clear conflict, the "case" is really a personal grievance without legal merit — the AI teammate says so directly, thanks the caller for reaching out, and closes the loop. The attorney's calendar stays clean. The fit calls that do get scheduled are ones the attorney actually wants to take.

The firm's win rate on fit calls goes up sharply because fit-call volume is being controlled at the front door, not being padded by matters that never should have gotten past intake.

How this fits into a small firm's day

The most immediate change a small firm feels when an AI intake teammate goes live is the phone stops being an interruption. Calls get answered live. They get handled. The attorney is not being pulled out of a case file to have a five-minute conversation that turns into thirty. The paralegal is not being asked to take a message they know will not get returned in time.

The attorney's calendar reflects real fit calls only. Each one comes with a pre-populated intake summary in the case management system — Clio, MyCase, PracticePanther, Smokeball, or whatever the firm runs. The attorney reads the summary, walks into the call knowing the matter, and either signs the engagement letter or does not. Signed engagement letters get triggered as case-opening tasks in the case management system without the attorney having to remember to kick off the process.

Wrong-fit matters get a clean, respectful decline in the moment — which is actually good for the firm's reputation. Callers who could not be helped remember being treated well and not having their time wasted. Referrals to and from other firms get logged and tracked, which creates a real referral network over time.

For an SMB law firm competing against larger firms with dedicated intake staff, this is the operational gap-close that used to require a full-time hire. Now it runs continuously and does not need to be managed.

Frequently asked questions

Does an AI intake handle sensitive matters — like PI or family — appropriately?

Yes. The intake teammate is trained on the tone and language your firm uses, including the level of empathy your practice area requires. For a personal injury call, the caller may be describing an accident that happened yesterday and be visibly upset. The AI teammate handles that with the calm, patient, one-question-at-a-time approach a trained intake specialist would use — not a rushed checklist. For a family law call involving a spouse or children, the AI teammate is trained to ask only what is necessary and to route the caller to the attorney quickly if there are safety or urgency concerns. The intake conversations are as human as the firm's script and training make them, and the AI teammate is transparent about what it is when asked.

Can we control which cases get scheduled versus disqualified?

Yes, and this is one of the more important configuration decisions during onboarding. Your firm defines the fit criteria — jurisdiction, practice area, matter type, minimum damages, statute-of-limitations windows, conflict checks — and the AI teammate applies those criteria consistently. If the criteria change (you open a new jurisdiction, you stop taking a certain matter type, you decide to be more selective on damages), the config is updated and the change takes effect immediately. You are always in control of which matters get in front of the attorney and which get declined at intake.

How does this handle unauthorized-practice-of-law concerns?

The intake teammate does not give legal advice, does not opine on the merits of a matter beyond fit checks, does not quote fees, and does not create an attorney-client relationship. It gathers facts and runs pre-defined fit criteria. That is intake, not practice. The teammate is trained to defer any question that starts to look like legal advice to the attorney directly — and if a caller pushes for an opinion, the AI teammate declines cleanly and offers to schedule the fit call so the attorney can address it. Every state's Rules of Professional Conduct treat intake as a permissible non-attorney function; this is the same principle a trained intake specialist would operate under.
